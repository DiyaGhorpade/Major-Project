# Audit of the Greenhouse Plant Growth Metrics EDA

## Overall assessment

The notebook has a clear exploratory clustering storyline and explains many steps well. However, it is not yet an effective general EDA, and it cannot support claims about plant health or prediction in its current form. It moves from a minimal data-quality check directly into K-Means, leaving the experimental design, distributions, feature relationships, outliers, and the supplied `Class` labels largely unexplored.

The most important correction is conceptual: the clusters are patterns in the same measurements used to construct them. They are not independently observed health categories. The notebook can currently describe multivariate growth profiles, but it cannot determine which profile is healthy or prove that the selected features predict health.

## Detailed shortcomings and remedies

### 1. Reproducibility and notebook state

The `df.head()` cell should display five rows, but its stored output ends with `[30000 rows x 14 columns]`. This indicates that the code was edited after the output was generated. Execution counts are also not a clean consecutive sequence: some cells have no count, while the final plotting cell has a much later count.

This is more than presentation polish. A reader cannot be sure that the elbow plot, cluster counts, means, and box plots were generated by the currently visible code.

**How to address it**

1. Restart the notebook kernel.
2. Clear all saved outputs.
3. Run all cells from the first cell to the last without manual reordering.
4. Resolve every warning or error rather than continuing past it.
5. Confirm that `df.head()` shows five rows and execution counts are consecutive.
6. Record package versions or provide an environment file if the notebook must be reproducible elsewhere.

### 2. Research question and target mismatch

The notebook mixes three different goals:

- describing current plant measurements;
- discovering unsupervised growth profiles; and
- predicting future growth or health.

Clustering addresses only the second goal. It does not demonstrate prediction because no held-out outcome is predicted. It also does not establish health because no health variable is present. Most numerical columns appear to be current growth measurements, so they may be outcomes of plant development rather than early predictors of it.

**How to address it**

State one primary question before analysis. Examples include:

- **Descriptive:** How do current plant and root measurements vary across experimental groups?
- **Unsupervised:** Are there stable, recurring combinations of vegetative and root development?
- **Predictive:** Can measurements at time `t` predict leaf area, biomass, yield, or health at a later time?

For prediction, define the target, prediction time, observation unit, and features available before that time. Use an independent health label if health is the target. Do not create “healthy/unhealthy” labels directly from the same features that will later predict them.

### 3. The experimental design is not explored

The supplied data contain:

- six `Class` groups with exactly 5,000 observations each; and
- three `Random` groups with 10,045 (`R1`), 9,959 (`R2`), and 9,996 (`R3`) observations.

Every `Random`–`Class` combination contains roughly 1,625–1,706 observations. This balanced pattern strongly suggests structured experimental metadata, although its exact meaning must be confirmed from the source.

Ignoring these fields risks interpreting a known treatment, condition, genotype, or block effect as a newly discovered natural cluster. Conversely, feeding them directly into K-Means would also be inappropriate because they are categorical and could dominate the intended feature-based exploration.

**How to address it**

- Obtain definitions for `S`, `T`, `A`, `B`, `C`, and `Random`/`R1–R3`.
- Plot and tabulate every feature by `Class` and, where relevant, by `Random`.
- Use standardized group-mean heatmaps and confidence intervals to summarize differences.
- Test whether `Random` represents a block, batch, replicate, greenhouse, or repeated measurement source.
- After clustering, attach labels to a copy of the original table and calculate normalized cross-tabulations with both metadata fields.
- Optionally use adjusted Rand index or normalized mutual information as descriptive agreement measures. These do not prove biological validity.

### 4. Data quality checks stop at missingness

The notebook correctly establishes that there are no null values and that the 12 measurements are numeric. It does not examine:

- fully duplicated or near-duplicated records;
- impossible or suspicious values;
- zero/near-zero variance;
- extreme observations;
- skewness and multimodality;
- measurement units;
- whether rows are independent plants or repeated measurements; or
- whether the data are simulated, augmented, or sampled over time.

With 30,000 rows, even modest dependence or duplication could make patterns look more stable than they really are.

**How to address it**

Add a quality-control section containing:

```python
df.shape
df.duplicated().sum()
df.nunique().sort_values()
df[feature_cols].describe(percentiles=[.01, .05, .25, .5, .75, .95, .99]).T
```

Then inspect histograms and box plots, preferably split by `Class`. Define valid minimum and maximum values using domain knowledge and units. If multiple rows belong to the same plant, the plant identifier—not the row—must determine train/test or resampling splits.

### 5. Essential descriptive EDA is missing

The notebook does not show the central tendency, spread, range, distribution shape, or pairwise relationships of the features before applying clustering. This makes it difficult to tell whether K-Means is discovering meaningful structure, simple skewness, outliers, or highly correlated measurement families.

**How to address it**

Add, in this order:

1. a descriptive-statistics table with units;
2. histograms/KDE plots for all features;
3. box or violin plots by `Class` and `Random`;
4. a Spearman correlation heatmap, since biological measurements may be nonlinear or skewed;
5. selected scatter plots for strongly related and scientifically important pairs, colored by `Class`;
6. a sampled pair plot or PCA view rather than plotting all pairwise combinations for 30,000 rows; and
7. written observations after each figure that distinguish facts from hypotheses.

High correlations would indicate redundancy. For example, wet and dry root weights may partly measure the same underlying root biomass. This matters because correlated features receive repeated influence in Euclidean distance and can disproportionately shape K-Means.

### 6. Preprocessing needs clearer safeguards

Standardization is appropriate because the feature scales differ greatly. However:

- `df.iloc[:, 1:-1]` depends on column order;
- assigning `Cluster` to a slice may cause view/copy ambiguity;
- the transformed array loses column labels; and
- replacing infinity with `100000` would create extreme artificial observations and seriously distort K-Means.

**How to address it**

Use explicit columns and fail visibly if non-finite values occur:

```python
feature_cols = [
    'ACHP', 'PHR', 'AWWGV', 'ALAP', 'ANPL', 'ARD',
    'ADWR', 'PDMVG', 'ARL', 'AWWR', 'ADWV', 'PDMRG'
]

X = df[feature_cols].copy()
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

if not np.isfinite(X_scaled).all():
    raise ValueError('Non-finite values found; diagnose the source before clustering.')
```

Assess outliers before scaling and compare `StandardScaler` with `RobustScaler` as a sensitivity analysis. Do not silently replace unexplained infinities with arbitrary large values.

### 7. The number and stability of clusters are not validated

Inertia always improves as `K` increases, and the visual elbow is subjective. A hard-coded `optimal_k = 3` is acceptable as a provisional exploration, but not as evidence that three natural groups exist.

K-Means also assumes compact groups under Euclidean distance. It can impose clusters even when the data form gradients, unequal-density groups, non-spherical structures, or no clear groups at all.

**How to address it**

- Print the metric table for every candidate `K` rather than relying only on a plot.
- Compare inertia, silhouette score, Calinski–Harabasz score, and Davies–Bouldin score.
- Because silhouette computation can be costly, use a documented random sample from the 30,000 rows.
- Repeat clustering across seeds and bootstrap/resampled datasets; compare assignments or centroids.
- Inspect cluster sizes and reject solutions dominated by implausibly tiny groups unless scientifically justified.
- Compare K-Means with at least one alternative suited to the observed geometry, such as Gaussian mixtures or hierarchical clustering on a sample.
- Use PCA as a visualization aid and report explained variance. A visually overlapping PCA plot does not automatically invalidate high-dimensional clusters, but it helps reveal obvious problems.

### 8. Cluster summaries are incomplete

The mean table is useful, but means alone can hide overlap, skewness, and outliers. The current expression also includes the numeric `Cluster` column in its own grouped mean, producing the meaningless values 0, 1, and 2. The manually created `cluster_0`, `cluster_1`, and `cluster_2` DataFrames are not used.

**How to address it**

```python
analysis_df = df.copy()
analysis_df['Cluster'] = labels

cluster_summary = analysis_df.groupby('Cluster')[feature_cols].agg(
    ['count', 'mean', 'median', 'std']
)
cluster_means = analysis_df.groupby('Cluster')[feature_cols].mean()
```

Also show standardized centroid profiles as a heatmap, confidence intervals, and distribution plots. Report the percentage of the dataset in every cluster. Remove unused per-cluster variables unless they support a specific later analysis.

### 9. Feature-importance claims are circular

The notebook selects `AWWGV`, `ARD`, `ADWR`, `ARL`, and `AWWR` by visually inspecting how well they separate clusters. Because those same features were inputs to K-Means, separation is expected. This can identify variables that describe the fitted solution, but not variables that independently predict plant health.

The mean table also suggests that omitted features such as `ALAP`, `PDMVG`, `PDMRG`, and `PHR` may strongly distinguish some profiles. A five-feature visual shortlist is therefore not reproducible or necessarily complete.

**How to address it**

- Rank absolute standardized centroid differences.
- Report effect sizes and uncertainty, not only p-values; with 30,000 rows, tiny differences can be statistically significant.
- If training a model to reproduce cluster labels, describe the result as cluster explanation and use held-out data.
- Use permutation importance or a simple decision tree only as a transparent approximation of cluster membership.
- Test predictive importance again using an independent future-growth or health target before making practical recommendations.

### 10. Biological interpretation is too confident

The current numerical results show trade-offs rather than a universal health ordering. For example, Cluster 2 is high on several root and leaf-related measurements but low on `PDMRG`; Cluster 1 is low on several root measurements but high on `ACHP`, `AWWGV`, `PDMVG`, and `ADWV`; Cluster 0 leads on `PDMRG`. None can be declared globally best without knowing units, developmental stage, treatment goals, and a health outcome.

Possible causes such as disease, poor drainage, nutrient deficiency, or light deficiency are not measured in the dataset. Listing them as explanations risks converting speculation into apparent findings.

**How to address it**

Use neutral names such as “root-dominant profile,” “vegetative/dry-matter profile,” or simply “Cluster 0–2” until validated. Phrase mechanisms as hypotheses requiring additional variables or experiments. Have a domain expert confirm whether high or low values are beneficial at the relevant developmental stage.

### 11. Plotting and code-quality issues

Several imports are unused (`OneHotEncoder`, `OrdinalEncoder`, `Pipeline`, `PCA`, and `re`), while `matplotlib`, `seaborn`, and `numpy` are imported twice. `print(df.info())` prints a redundant `None`. The box-plot grid lacks `tight_layout()` and an explicit `show()`, and abbreviations make axes harder to interpret.

**How to address it**

- Keep one import cell containing only used libraries.
- Call `df.info()` directly.
- Add readable labels containing names and units.
- Add `plt.tight_layout()` and `plt.show()`.
- Consider hiding fliers or using sampled violin/boxen plots only after separately documenting outliers.
- Write reusable plotting functions instead of repeating manual layout logic as the EDA grows.

## Recommended effective EDA workflow

The notebook would be stronger if reorganized into the following sequence:

1. **Question and observation unit** — define what one row represents and distinguish description, clustering, and prediction.
2. **Data dictionary and experiment design** — document feature names, units, `Class`, `Random`, treatments, replicates, and collection timing.
3. **Reproducible load and integrity checks** — shape, head, types, missingness, duplicates, uniqueness, ranges, and assertions.
4. **Univariate EDA** — descriptive statistics and distributions for every measurement.
5. **Group-aware EDA** — compare features across `Class` and `Random`, with effect sizes and uncertainty.
6. **Relationship EDA** — correlations, selected scatter plots, redundancy, and biologically motivated ratios only where units support them.
7. **Clustering as an optional secondary analysis** — scaled features, several values of `K`, validation metrics, stability checks, and alternative models.
8. **External interpretation** — compare clusters with experimental metadata and any independent outcomes.
9. **Predictive formulation** — only after defining a target, time ordering, leakage-safe split, baseline, metrics, and validation scheme.
10. **Conclusions** — separate observed evidence, reasonable inference, unresolved questions, and proposed next experiments.

## Minimum standard for revised conclusions

A defensible conclusion from the current data would be:

> The numerical measurements contain several recurring multivariate growth profiles, with coordinated differences in root, leaf, chlorophyll, and vegetative dry-matter measures. A provisional three-cluster K-Means solution summarizes these profiles, but its stability and relationship to the six experimental classes have not yet been established. No cluster can currently be identified as healthy or unhealthy because the dataset does not include an independently validated health outcome.

This wording retains the useful discovery while keeping the scientific claim proportional to the evidence.
